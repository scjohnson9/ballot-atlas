faq:
  - question: What would HJR 1 change about current Virginia law?
    answer: |-
      Virginia's current law permits abortion during the first and second trimesters through statute ([Va. Code § 18.2-73](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/18.2-73/)). Third-trimester abortions are restricted, requiring the certification of three physicians that continuation of the pregnancy would substantially and irremediably impair the mental or physical health of the pregnant woman ([Va. Code § 18.2-74](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/18.2-74/)).

      If HJR 1 is approved, abortion will move from being permitted by legal statute to being considered a person's ["fundamental right"](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) enshrined in the state's constitution. Existing statutory restrictions—including the current three-physician certification for third-trimester abortions—would be constitutionally vulnerable and likely struck down.

      Removing something from Virginia's state constitution is very difficult. It would require the General Assembly to pass a new amendment in two consecutive sessions with an intervening House of Delegates election, followed by voter ratification.
  - question: How does the measure define "reproductive freedom"?
    answer: |-
      According to the amendment text, "reproductive freedom" is ["the ability to make and carry out decisions relating to one's own prenatal care, childbirth, postpartum care, contraception, abortion care, miscarriage management, and fertility care."](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF)

      Notice how the amendment groups abortion alongside prenatal care, childbirth, and other legitimate categories of medical care. This rhetorical maneuver conflates the deliberate ending of an unborn life with the ordinary care of pregnant women and their children, so that voters process them as equivalent forms of healthcare.
  - question: Why does the measure refer to abortion as "care"?
    answer: |-
      Throughout the amendment, abortion is repeatedly described as ["abortion care,"](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) placed in a list alongside "prenatal care," "postpartum care," "contraception," "miscarriage management," and "fertility care."

      This language is not accidental. By treating the ending of an unborn life as a form of "care," the amendment obscures what is actually taking place—not the care of a life, but the deliberate ending of one. It is a rhetorical maneuver that softens the reality of abortion and conflates it with legitimate medical treatment aimed at healing and preserving life.
  - question: How does the measure define "viability"?
    answer: |-
      [HJR 1](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) does not define "viability." The word appears only once in the amendment text, in the third-trimester exception: ["when in the professional judgment of a physician the fetus is not viable."](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF)

      The amendment provides no gestational-age standard, no medical criteria, and no reference to accepted clinical benchmarks. Whether an unborn child is deemed viable is left entirely to "the professional judgment of a physician."

      Grounding the viability of an unborn child in an individual physician's opinion has grave consequences. If he wants the child to be non-viable, then he can rule that way. If he wants the child to be viable, he can rule that way.
  - question: Would the amendment allow late-term abortions?
    answer: |-
      Yes. Even in the third trimester, [the amendment](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) states that no abortion may be prohibited when a physician judges it ["medically indicated to protect the life or physical or mental health of the pregnant individual."](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF)

      Because health is not defined in the measure, it includes the possibility that a late-term abortion of a baby who could survive outside the womb could be administered on the basis of a woman's mental health—as decided by the professional opinion of a single physician. Combined with the amendment's undefined viability standard, the third-trimester regulation it appears to permit is largely illusory.
  - question: What does "compelling state interest" mean under this measure?
    answer: |-
      What exactly qualifies as a compelling state interest is narrowly limited. According to [the amendment text](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF), a state interest is compelling ["only if it is for the limited purpose of maintaining or improving the health of an individual seeking care, consistent with accepted clinical standards of care and evidence-based medicine, and does not infringe on that individual's autonomous decision making."](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF)

      Notice that this definition is narrowly limited to the interests of the one seeking the abortion. It would constitutionally exclude the pro-life movement from citing the health of the unborn child as a compelling state interest to curb abortions.
  - question: Does the measure require parental consent for minors?
    answer: |-
      This amendment is silent on minors seeking abortions. They are treated just like any other individual seeking an abortion. Virginia's current parental involvement law would be constitutionally vulnerable if HJR 1 is approved, since a minor's exercise of a "fundamental right" cannot be ["directly or indirectly, denied, burdened, or infringed upon"](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) except by a compelling state interest.

      Sadly, this measure makes children more vulnerable to sexual abuse and human trafficking. Consider that an abuser could impregnate a child, drive the child to an abortion clinic, and force that child to get an abortion—without the child's parents ever knowing.
  - question: Would the measure affect public funding of abortions?
    answer: |-
      Virginia currently limits public funding of abortions to cases of rape, incest, life endangerment, or severe fetal anomaly. HJR 1 does not explicitly address public funding.

      However, the amendment prohibits the Commonwealth from ["directly or indirectly"](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) denying, burdening, or infringing the right, and requires that the Commonwealth "not discriminate in the protection or enforcement of this fundamental right." Courts could reasonably interpret this language to require public funding of abortions across all categories, effectively striking down current funding limits.
  - question: Does the measure protect those who assist others in obtaining an abortion?
    answer: |-
      Yes. [HJR 1](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) includes a distinct clause shielding those who help another person obtain an abortion: ["The Commonwealth shall not penalize, prosecute, or otherwise take adverse action against any individual for aiding or assisting another individual in exercising such other individual's right to reproductive freedom with such other individual's voluntary consent."](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF)

      This protection is broader than similar amendments in other states. Out-of-state helpers—including telehealth providers, transportation networks, and abortion funds—would be shielded from Virginia legal consequence for their role in obtaining an abortion. Under this clause, an abuser who drives a minor to an abortion clinic, or an out-of-state fund that pays for the procedure, could not be prosecuted by the Commonwealth.
  - question: Why does Virginia require a second vote on constitutional amendments?
    answer: |-
      [HJR 1](https://lis.blob.core.windows.net/files/1081212.PDF) is a legislatively-referred constitutional amendment. Under [Article XII, Section 1 of the Virginia Constitution](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/constitution/article12/section1/), a proposed amendment must:

      1. Be approved by a majority of both houses of the General Assembly;
      2. Be approved again, in identical form, by the next General Assembly session held after a House of Delegates election; and
      3. Be ratified by a majority of Virginia voters at a general election.

      HJR 1 received its first General Assembly approval during the 2025 session. Following the November 2025 House of Delegates election, the measure was reintroduced during the 2026 session for a second approval. If it receives second General Assembly approval and voter ratification on November 3, 2026, the amendment becomes part of the Virginia Constitution.

      The necessary threshold for voter approval is 50%.
