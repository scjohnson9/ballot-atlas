faq:
  - question: What would Question 2 change about Virginia's current constitution?
    answer: |-
      Virginia's current constitution ([Article I, Section 15-A](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/constitution/article1/section15-A/)) defines marriage as "only a union between one man and one woman." This provision, known as the Marshall-Newman Amendment, was approved by 57% of Virginia voters in 2006.

      [Question 2](https://www.elections.virginia.gov/election-law/proposed-constitutional-amendment-question-2/) would remove this definition from the Virginia Constitution and replace it with language establishing marriage as a right "regardless of the sex, gender, or race" of the two adult persons seeking to marry.

      If passed, Virginia would be required by its own constitution to recognize and treat equally "any lawful marriage between two adult persons."
  - question: What is Virginia's current constitutional definition of marriage?
    answer: |-
      Virginia's constitutional definition of marriage is set out in [Article I, Section 15-A](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/constitution/article1/section15-A/), known as the Marshall-Newman Amendment. It reads: "That only a union between one man and one woman may be a marriage valid in or recognized by this Commonwealth and its political subdivisions."

      The amendment further prohibits the state from creating or recognizing "a legal status for relationships of unmarried individuals that intends to approximate the design, qualities, significance, or effects of marriage"—language understood at the time to also prevent recognition of civil unions and domestic partnerships.

      The Marshall-Newman Amendment was approved by Virginia voters in a 2006 referendum, with 57% voting "yes."
  - question: Isn't same-sex marriage already legal in Virginia under Obergefell v. Hodges?
    answer: |-
      Yes, same-sex marriages are currently performed and recognized in Virginia—but on two different legal foundations.

      **At the statutory level**, Virginia's statutes prohibiting same-sex marriage ([Va. Code §§ 20-45.2 and 20-45.3](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/20-45.2/)) were repealed by the General Assembly in 2020. State law no longer bans same-sex marriage.

      **At the constitutional level**, however, [Article I, Section 15-A](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/constitution/article1/section15-A/)—the Marshall-Newman Amendment—still defines marriage as between one man and one woman. This provision is currently unenforceable because of the U.S. Supreme Court's 2015 ruling in [_Obergefell v. Hodges_](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/14pdf/14-556_3204.pdf), which held that same-sex couples have a federal constitutional right to marry.

      If the Supreme Court were to overturn or narrow _Obergefell_—as [Justice Thomas signaled openness to](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf) in his concurrence to _Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization_ (2022)—Virginia's constitutional ban would immediately become enforceable again. Question 2 would remove that constitutional provision and replace it with affirmative constitutional protection for same-sex marriage.
  - question: What specific language would Question 2 add to the Virginia Constitution?
    answer: |-
      [Question 2](https://www.elections.virginia.gov/election-law/proposed-constitutional-amendment-question-2/) would replace the Marshall-Newman Amendment with new language declaring that "marriage is one of the vital personal rights essential to the orderly pursuit of happiness."

      It would then prohibit the state from denying "the issuance of a marriage license to two adult persons seeking a lawful marriage on the basis of the sex, gender, or race of such persons."

      Finally, it would require the state to "recognize any lawful marriage between two adult persons and treat such marriages equally under the law, regardless of the sex, gender, or race of such persons."

      The amendment contains no explicit religious liberty protections or carve-outs.
  - question: Why does the amendment include "race" alongside sex and gender?
    answer: |-
      The inclusion of "race" carries particular symbolic weight for Virginia. In 1967, the United States Supreme Court ruled in [_Loving v. Virginia_](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/388/1/) that state bans on interracial marriage were unconstitutional—a case that originated with a Virginia couple, Richard and Mildred Loving. Supporters of Question 2 argue that constitutionally prohibiting race-based marriage denials rights a historical wrong.

      The bundling of "race" with "sex" and "gender" has, however, drawn criticism. Interracial marriage protections are not seriously in dispute today—unlike the question of same-sex marriage, which remains a matter of significant theological and civic debate. Opponents argue that including race in the amendment may be designed to make a "no" vote appear to oppose protections voters overwhelmingly support, when in fact the central contested question is about redefining marriage itself.
  - question: Could religious institutions or clergy be required to perform same-sex marriages if Question 2 passes?
    answer: |-
      No. Under the First Amendment to the United States Constitution, religious institutions and clergy cannot be legally compelled to perform marriages that violate their religious convictions. This protection has been consistently upheld by courts, and Question 2 does not alter it.

      However, religious institutions have faced significant indirect pressure in other states and contexts that have adopted similar protections—including challenges to tax-exempt status, licensing of religious schools and adoption agencies, participation in public programs and contracts, and exposure of individual Christians (bakers, photographers, counselors) to civil penalties for declining to participate in same-sex weddings or ceremonies.

      Opponents of Question 2 argue that embedding a constitutional right to marry regardless of sex into Virginia's Bill of Rights would, over time, strengthen the legal footing for similar pressure on Virginia's religious institutions and individual believers.
  - question: Would this amendment affect religious liberty protections in Virginia?
    answer: |-
      Question 2 contains no explicit religious liberty protections or carve-outs. This stands in contrast to some other state marriage-protection measures and to the federal [Respect for Marriage Act (2022)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/117th-congress/house-bill/8404), both of which paired expanded marriage recognition with specific religious liberty language.

      Virginia's existing religious liberty protections—including First Amendment protections and state statutes safeguarding religious free exercise—remain in place and are not directly altered by Question 2. However, religious liberty concerns typically arise not from marriage recognition itself but from its downstream consequences: how religious schools, adoption agencies, counseling practices, business owners, and clergy are treated by state agencies and courts when their convictions on marriage conflict with newly-elevated constitutional rights.

      By enshrining the right to marry regardless of sex at the constitutional level without religious liberty carve-outs, Question 2 could tilt the legal balancing test in future religious liberty cases against those who hold to a traditional understanding of marriage.
  - question: Why is Virginia's General Assembly asking voters to remove this provision now?
    answer: |-
      The push to repeal the Marshall-Newman Amendment has intensified since the U.S. Supreme Court's 2022 ruling in [_Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization_](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf), which overturned _Roe v. Wade_. In his concurring opinion, Justice Clarence Thomas wrote that the Court "should reconsider all of this Court's substantive due process precedents, including… _Obergefell_." That signal led same-sex marriage advocates in several states to pursue state-level constitutional protections that would survive even if _Obergefell_ were overturned.

      In Virginia, this amendment (introduced as House Joint Resolution 9 and Senate Joint Resolution 249) passed both chambers of the General Assembly during the 2025 and 2026 sessions, meeting Virginia's two-session constitutional amendment requirement before being placed on the November 3, 2026 ballot.

      Supporters argue the amendment is necessary to safeguard existing marriages regardless of future federal rulings. Opponents argue that the current legal status quo—same-sex marriage protected by federal precedent and state statute—is already sufficient, and that constitutionally enshrining a redefinition of marriage permanently forecloses the possibility of restoring the historic understanding of marriage in Virginia law.
